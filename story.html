Is OLED really the thirstiest TV?

A first look says yes. A fairer comparison says the screen technology matters far less than the size of the screen.

Chapter 1: The first impression

OLED looks like the biggest energy user

Averaging every registered television by screen technology gives a clear ranking. OLED sits at the top and older LCD sets at the bottom.

It is tempting to stop here. OLED averages about 486 kWh a year, nearly 50% more than LCD at about 334 kWh.

Chapter 2: The hidden factor

Bigger screens use far more power

Each dot is one television model. The cloud climbs steadily as screens get larger, from under 100 kWh a year for the smallest sets to well over 1,000 for the biggest.

Screen size and energy use are strongly linked (correlation about 0.85 across all 5,036 registry rows). If one technology is mostly sold in large sizes, its average will look high for that reason alone.

Chapter 3: A fair comparison

Compare like with like

Splitting the televisions into Small, Medium and Large bands puts each technology against others of a similar size.

Size dominates. Across all technologies, Large TVs average about 743 kWh a year against about 158 for Small, roughly 4.7 times as much. In the Medium band the three technologies are almost level, between 384 and 406 kWh.

The one big gap is Small OLED (233 kWh against 135 for Small LCD), but it rests on only 19 models, so it is not strong evidence.

Chapter 4: Efficiency

Star ratings barely separate the technologies

The star rating index measures how efficient a television is, not how much power it uses in total. On that measure the three technologies are close together.

Average star rating index: LCD about 5.2, OLED about 5.0 and LCD (LED) about 4.9 on a scale that runs up to roughly 9 in this data.

What this means when buying a TV

  • Choose the size you actually need first. It has the biggest effect on the yearly energy bill.
  • Compare star ratings between televisions of the same size, rather than comparing technologies on average power.
  • Do not rule out OLED on energy grounds alone: at similar sizes the averages are close.

About this data

The figures come from the Australian Government Energy Rating registry (exported 30 September 2026). They describe registered models, not televisions sold or used in homes, and the energy values are labelled test figures rather than real household use. Averages can be pulled up by a few very large sets, and the Small OLED group is too small for firm conclusions.
